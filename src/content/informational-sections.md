# State of the Program
Some of you already know part of this story. For others, this will be new.

In mid- to late May, after nine years of treatment for sarcoma that included intensive radiation, my sister was diagnosed with an aggressive form of blood cancer. She needed a transplant. Several possible donors were tested. I was a perfect match on every measure the transplant team used, including stem cells and T cells.

I spent two weeks in Houston at MD Anderson to donate. The collection produced twice the transplant team’s target. Her transplant was successful and, as I write this, she is cancer-free.

Cohort 5 sent gifts and messages. Many students and alumni wrote letters. Luke brought paper copies to my house in late June. I am used to offering care in this community. Receiving it so generously was humbling, and I am grateful beyond what I can say here.

A little more than a week before I learned I would be the donor, my son graduated from the University of Virginia. At Valedictory Exercises, President Emeritus Jim Ryan gave the class four words: [“Do the right thing. Always.”](https://news.virginia.edu/content/valediction-jim-ryan-shares-simple-message-do-right-thing) I did not know how soon they would apply to me.

Leadership shows in acts like these: colleagues who take on work you cannot do, a cohort that sends gifts, people who write letters. This year many of you made that visible to me.

Alison and I are also, at last, in the home we have been building for almost four years, and we will welcome alumni there during the Annual Fall Retreat.
## Our collective work
Care in this community runs both ways. The people we teach and advise also teach and sustain us.

Across six cohorts, the Executive Ed.D. community now includes 89 students and alumni from more than 15 countries and regions across Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, the Middle East, and Australia. They work at research universities, liberal arts colleges, art and design schools, theological institutions, community colleges, and law schools, as presidents, provosts, vice presidents, deans, directors, faculty members, and senior staff. Our community’s research runs across nine connected fields:

- international higher education and global mobility;
- first-generation access and student success;
- institutional governance, leadership, and transformation;
- enrollment strategy and institutional finance;
- equity, diversity, and BIPOC leadership;
- faculty and academic workforce development;
- technology, AI, and digital innovation;
- policy, credentials, and workforce alignment; and
- health professions and student well-being.

The issues overlap. Questions of technology are questions of human agency; questions of finance are questions of mission and access.

This year Cohort 3 defended and graduated, Cohort 6 brought new senior leaders into the program, and the community met at Boston College, in Santiago, and at Connors. Alumni grew through an executive dialogue, three rounds of group coaching, planning for new roundtables, and returns to program events, with the October retreat ahead. We also welcomed [Jessica Belue Buckley](https://www.bc.edu/content/bc-web/schools/lynch-school/faculty-research/faculty-directory/jessica-belue-buckley.html) to Boston College as Associate Professor of Higher Education; she teaches Foundations of Social Justice in Higher Education this fall.
## The long work of leading well
This issue takes its theme from the phrase that now organizes much of my thinking: the long work of leading well. Higher education is under pressure to answer fast, to demographic decline, funding cuts, political scrutiny, and artificial intelligence, and answers made at that speed tend to serve the next quarter. The leadership this moment needs is strategic, future-focused, and for others: it reads the structure beneath the headline, thinks in decades rather than semesters, and takes its bearings from the students, faculty, and communities an institution exists to serve. It is formed over years, in relationships, and it holds up only if it is renewed.

The students and alumni in these pages are leaders of that kind. They drafted state regulations, took up provostships and vice presidencies, opened routes for students who had none, and kept public records that institutions need. They did this work while advising one another, returning for residencies and coaching, and writing letters to a director who needed them. That is the long work, and it is the reason a community like this one exists.

_Chris_
